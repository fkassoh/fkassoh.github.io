Why Real-World Robotics Is Hard
Three properties that don't show up in a board game
Nondeterministic & partially observable
● Motors slip, wheels drift, sensors are noisy — the robot never knows its exact state.
● Contrast with Chess or Go: perfectly known board, perfectly reliable moves.
Continuous & high-dimensional
● State and action spaces aren't a handful of discrete choices — they're real-valued vectors.
● A humanoid robot can have hundreds of joints, each a continuous degree of freedom.

26.2 ROBOT HARDWARE — SENSING
Proprioceptive Sensors: Sensing Yourself
● Proprioceptive sensors report the robot's own motion and internal state — not the outside world.
– Shaft decoders — measure a motor shaft's angular position; on wheels, this gives odometry (distance traveled).
– Odometry drifts: wheels slip and skid, so it's accurate only over short distances.
– Inertial sensors (gyroscopes) — resist changes in velocity, reducing drift between fixes.
– Force & torque sensors — critical when handling fragile or unknown objects; can sample hundreds of times per second.
WHY THIS MATTERS
Picture a one-ton manipulator screwing in a light bulb: without force sensing, it's trivially easy to apply too much torque and shatter the bulb. Force/torque feedback is what lets a robot feel how hard it's gripping or turning.

Cooperative or Competitive? It Depends on the Moment
COOPERATIVE MOMENT
In a narrow hospital corridor, Wren and Nurse Priya both want to avoid a collision — their goals align, so this is genuinely cooperative.
COMPETITIVE MOMENT
But both also want to get through quickly. A robot that is always maximally polite and always yields can get stuck forever in a crowded hallway and never reach Maria Santos's room.
REAL-WORLD ANCHOR
This is exactly why early “always-yield” hospital and airport robots frustrated staff — over-polite robots would freeze in busy hallways. Modern designs (and Waymo's highway merges) intentionally model a small competitive component so the robot asserts itself just enough to make progress.

26.3 FRAMING THE PROBLEM
Whose Reward Function Is It, Anyway?
● A robot is usually acting in service of a human — delivering a meal to a hospital patient is for the patient's benefit, not the robot's.
● The true reward function lives with the end user, not the robot designer.
● In practice: an engineer specifies a proxy reward and hopes it approximates what the user actually wants — or the robot has to decipher the user's desires directly.
NORTHGATE HOSPITAL EXAMPLE
Wren's designer might code the proxy reward “minimize delivery time.” But Maria Santos's actual reward function includes “don't startle me,” “don't block Nurse Priya mid-procedure,” and “don't drop the tray.” A robot that only optimizes the coded proxy can satisfy the letter of its reward while completely missing the point — this exact gap motivates Section 26.8's discussion of learning what humans want.

26.3 FRAMING THE PROBLEM
The Cost of Decoupling
● Splitting the problem into layers reduces complexity — but each split gives up an opportunity for the pieces to help each other.
– Action can improve perception, and determine what perception is even useful to gather.
– A motion-level decision might look great in isolation but be terrible once you account for how it will actually be tracked by the controller.
– A task-level plan can turn out to be uninstantiable once motion planning tries to execute it.
● The trend in modern robotics: reintegrate. Motion planning + control together; task + motion planning together; perception + prediction + action, closing the loop.

26.4 ROBOTIC PERCEPTION
The Recursive Filtering Equation
GENERAL
P(Xt+1 | z1:t+1, a1:t) = α · P(zt+1 | Xt+1) · ∫ P(Xt+1 | xt, at) P(xt | z1:t, a1:t−1) dxt
SUBSTITUTE
The integral folds the previous belief P(xt | z1:t, a1:t−1) forward through the motion model P(Xt+1 | xt, at), then the sensor model P(zt+1 | Xt+1) re-weights it against the new observation.
RESULT
A recursive update: yesterday's belief + today's action + today's observation → today's belief. Sum becomes an integral because the state is continuous.

26.4.1 LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING
Defining the Robot's Pose
The state vector
● Xt = (xt, yt, θt)ᵀ — two Cartesian coordinates plus a heading angle.
● Kinematic action = an instantaneous (vt, ωt): translational and rotational velocity.
Deterministic motion model
● X̂t+1 = Xt + (vtΔt·cos θt, vtΔt·sin θt, ωtΔt)
● In reality, this prediction is noisy — modeled as Gaussian: P(Xt+1|Xt,vt,ωt) = N(X̂t+1, Σx)
R1 GROUNDING
For our warehouse arm R1's mobile base, moving forward at velocity v for a tenth of a second while turning slightly changes x, y, and θ by exactly this formula. The Gaussian wrapped around it says: R1's wheels will drift a little, and we quantify exactly how much.

26.4.1 LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING
Two Sensor Models: Landmarks vs. Range Scans
Landmark model
● Sense a known, identifiable feature at (xi, yi); predict its range and bearing by simple geometry: ẑt = h(xt).
● Add Gaussian noise: P(zt|xt) = N(ẑt, Σz).
● Advantage: instant localization if a landmark is visible.
● Weakness: you must first correctly identify which landmark you're seeing (the data-association problem).
Range-scan (beam) model
● A sensor array reports M range values along fixed bearings — no landmark identification needed.
● P(zt|xt) = α·∏ e^(−(zj−ẑj)²/2σ²) — assumes independent, identically distributed beam noise.
● Works even facing a featureless wall — but is less immediately informative than a clear landmark sighting.
WHICH DOES WREN USE?
Wren's lidar naturally produces range-scan data — a full sweep of distances with no need to first recognize a specific hallway landmark.

26.4.1 LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING
Kalman Filter Localization & Linearization
The core idea
● Represent the belief as ONE Gaussian: mean μt, covariance Σt.
● Problem: Gaussians stay Gaussian only under linear motion/sensor models. Real f and h are nonlinear.
● Fix: linearize f and h locally with a first-degree Taylor expansion — tangent to the true function at μt.
● A Kalman filter using this trick is called an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF).
landmark sighted
uncertainty grows while moving blind, shrinks after a good observation

26.4.1 LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING
SLAM: Simultaneous Localization and Mapping
THE CHICKEN-AND-EGG PROBLEM
What if no map exists yet? The robot must localize itself relative to a map it doesn't fully know, while simultaneously building that map — using a location estimate it doesn't fully trust.
● EKF-SLAM: augment the state vector with landmark locations too, then run the same EKF machinery.
– The EKF update scales quadratically — feasible for small maps (a few hundred landmarks), painful for large ones.
– Richer, larger maps: graph relaxation methods (recall Bayesian network inference, Ch. 13) or Expectation–Maximization (Ch. 20–21).
REAL-WORLD ANCHOR
This is the exact technique behind every robot vacuum's “smart mapping” feature — the first time it's placed in a new home, it is solving SLAM in real time, one lidar or bump sensor sweep at a time.

26.5 PLANNING & CONTROL
Path vs. Trajectory
Path
● A sequence of points in geometric space — “go from A to B to C.”
● Finding a good path = motion planning.
Trajectory
● A path with a TIME attached to each point — “start at A, take 1s to reach B, 1.5s more to reach C.”
● Executing a trajectory = trajectory tracking control.
WHY THE SPLIT MATTERS
We separate the question “what path avoids obstacles?” from “what forces/torques make the robot actually follow it?” This mirrors the plans-vs-policies distinction from Chapters 3 and 16, and we'll return to that exact analogy later in this section.

26.5.1 CONFIGURATION SPACE
Workspace vs. Configuration Space
WORKSPACE W
obstacle O
R
R can translate freely; must avoid O
CONFIGURATION SPACE C
Cobs
single point = full pose
The robot IS a point in C; Cobs shrinks it to the boundary
GENERAL
A robot's body is a set of (x,y) points in the workspace. Avoiding an obstacle means checking that NO point on the robot overlaps NO point on the obstacle.
SUBSTITUTE
Instead, represent the ENTIRE robot as a single point in an abstract space — the configuration space, or C-space — using its known size/shape plus its current pose.
RESULT
Motion planning becomes: find a path for one point, in C, that avoids the C-space obstacle Cobs. Vastly simpler than juggling every point on the robot's body.

26.5.1 CONFIGURATION SPACE
C-space Obstacles Can Be Strange Shapes
● Even simple, flat polygonal obstacles in the workspace can become highly nonlinear — even concave — shapes in C-space.
– For a two-link arm, one vertical obstacle hanging from the ceiling creates a warped C-space region that's hard to recognize at first glance.
– The dark region surrounding all of C-space in Fig. 26.12(b) is itself an obstacle: configurations where the robot collides with ITSELF.
● Practical consequence: in practice, we almost never construct Cobs explicitly. We probe it.
COLLISION CHECKER
A function γ(q) → {0,1}: does configuration q collide with something, yes or no? It's far easier to test one configuration at a time than to build the full obstacle region up front — this single function underlies almost every motion planner in the rest of this section.

26.5.2 MOTION PLANNING
The Piano Mover's Problem
WHERE THE NAME COMES FROM
A mover's struggle to get a large, irregular piano from one room to another without hitting anything — the formal name for the general motion planning problem.
● Given: a workspace W, an obstacle region O ⊂ W, a robot with C-space C and body points A(q), a start qs, and a goal qg.
● Find a continuous path τ(t), with τ(0) = qs and τ(1) = qg, that stays entirely inside Cfree for every t ∈ [0,1].
● The parameter t is NOT time — it's just “how far along the path,” a number from 0 to 1.
– Can be made harder: goal sets instead of single points, workspace-defined goals, cost minimization, or constraints (keep a carried cup upright!).

26.5.2 TRAJECTORY OPTIMIZATION
Worked Derivation: Why Straight Lines Are Optimal
GENERAL
Euler-Lagrange equation: for J[τ] = ∫F(s,τ(s),τ̇(s))ds, the gradient is ∇τJ(s) = ∂F/∂τ(s) − d/dt[∂F/∂τ̇(s)].
SUBSTITUTE
For efficiency alone, F(s,τ(s),τ̇(s)) = ‖τ̇(s)‖². This F doesn't depend on τ(s) directly, so the first term is 0, leaving ∇τJ(s) = −τ̈(s).
RESULT
Setting the gradient to 0 gives τ̈(s)=0. Integrate twice: τ(s) = a·s + b. That's a STRAIGHT LINE — the most efficient path when there are no obstacles at all.

26.5.3 TRAJECTORY TRACKING CONTROL
From Path to Torque: Kinematic vs. Dynamic State
● Kinematic state: just the configuration q. Not enough to compute torque!
● Dynamic state: (q, q̇) — configuration AND velocity.
● The dynamics model f: q̈ = f(q, q̇, u), where u is a torque. The inverse f⁻¹ tells you what torque produces a desired acceleration.
● Retiming: our path τ(t) doesn't know about velocity, acceleration, or real elapsed time. We must convert it into a genuine trajectory ξ(t) over [0, T] before we can apply any of this.

Why Open-Loop Control Fails
OPEN-LOOP CONTROL LAW
u(t) = f⁻¹(ξ(t), ξ̇(t), ξ̈(t)) — compute the torque you THINK you need, from the plan alone, and just send it. No feedback at all.
● Works perfectly... if f is exact. It never is.
– Real systems can't measure mass and inertia exactly.
– f often ignores physical effects like stiction — the friction that resists getting a stationary surface moving.
– Errors accumulate over time with nothing to correct them — the robot drifts further and further from the reference path.

26.5.3 CONTROLLERS
The Proportional (P) Controller
GENERAL
u(t) = KP · (ξ(t) − qt) — push back against error, proportional to how large the error is.
SUBSTITUTE
KP=1.0 (strong correction) and KP=0.1 (gentle correction) are both tried on the same smooth reference path.
RESULT
BOTH oscillate — a P controller behaves like a spring, with no friction to damp it out. Stable, but NOT strictly stable.
Why lowering KP doesn't fix it
● Intuition says a gentler gain = gentler correction.
● But without friction, a P controller is mathematically a spring — it just oscillates FOREVER around the target, forever.

26.5.3 CONTROLLERS
The PD Controller: Adding Damping
GENERAL
u(t) = KP(ξ(t) − qt) + KD(ξ̇(t) − q̇t) — proportional term PLUS a derivative term on the rate of change of the error.
SUBSTITUTE
KP=0.3, KD=0.8 applied to the same reference path that made the P controller oscillate.
RESULT
A smooth, non-oscillating approach to the path — STRICTLY stable. Intuition: the derivative term counteracts rapid error changes, damping the system exactly like a shock absorber next to the spring.

26.5.3 CONTROLLERS
The PID Controller: Killing Persistent Bias
GENERAL
u(t) = KP(ξ(t)−qt) + KI∫₀ᵗ(ξ(s)−qs)ds + KD(ξ̇(t)−q̇t) — add an integral term over accumulated past error.
SUBSTITUTE
Example: an autonomous car on a banked road is systematically pulled to one side by an unmodeled force.
RESULT
The integral term keeps growing as long as error persists, forcing the correction ever stronger until the systematic bias is finally driven to zero.
The mnemonic
● P — try harder the farther away you are.
● D — try even harder if the error is INCREASING.
● I — try harder if you haven't made progress in a while.

26.5.3 CONTROLLERS

GENERAL

SUBSTITUTE

RESULT
Combines open-loop model knowledge with closed-loop error correction — the gains effectively adapt as the robot's configuration changes.

26.5.3 A CONCEPTUAL RESET
Plans vs. Policies — Same Analogy, New Costume
● The underlying MDP here: states are dynamic states (q, q̇); actions are control torques.
● Our P/PD/PID/computed-torque control laws ARE policies — they say what to do from any state you might land in.
● But they're far from OPTIMAL policies, because the true dynamic-state MDP is continuous and high-dimensional — computationally intractable to solve exactly.
● So instead we break the problem up: plan first in a simplified kinematic space (ignoring dynamics), THEN build a policy that tracks that plan and corrects for drift.
– Two sources of suboptimality: (1) planning without dynamics in mind, and (2) assuming the best response to drift is always “return to the original plan.”

SUBSTITUTE

One unified optimization, closer to what we saw in the search (Ch. 3) and MDP (Ch. 16) chapters — a sequence of actions if dynamics are known, or a policy if not.

26.5.4 OPTIMAL CONTROL
The Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)
THE MAGIC CASE
When the cost J is QUADRATIC (xᵀQx + uᵀRu) and the dynamics f are LINEAR (ẋ = Ax + Bu), the optimal policy has a closed form — NO value iteration, NO policy iteration, NO local optimization needed at all.
● The optimal value function ("cost to go") is quadratic; the optimal policy is exactly linear: u = −Kx.
– Finding K requires solving one algebraic Riccati equation — a single, direct calculation.
– Real problems rarely have exactly quadratic costs and linear dynamics — but LQR is used constantly anyway, because it's so cheap and reliable.

26.5.4 OPTIMAL CONTROL
ILQR: Bootstrapping LQR for Real Problems
● Iterative LQR (ILQR): start with some initial solution.
– At every iteration: compute a LINEAR approximation of the dynamics, and a QUADRATIC approximation of the cost, around the current trajectory.
– Solve THAT local LQR problem exactly, to get an improved trajectory.
– Repeat — each iteration should nudge you closer to a genuinely good, if not perfectly optimal, trajectory.
REAL-WORLD ANCHOR
ILQR-style methods sit at the core of much of modern legged-robot and manipulator control, including work that let simplified humanoid robots learn to walk and robot arms learn to hit a baseball.

26.6 UNCERTAIN MOVEMENTS
Where Determinism Quietly Breaks Down
● Uncertainty in robotics comes from partial observability AND stochastic (or unmodeled) action effects.
● Even approximation algorithms like particle filtering introduce their own error — you don't get an exact belief state, even in a perfectly modeled world.
● Most robots today cope with two deterministic shortcuts:
– Discretize the continuous space (visibility graphs, cell decomposition — Section 26.5).
– Pick the single MOST LIKELY state from the belief distribution, and just plan as if that's the true state.
THE RISK
The most-likely-state shortcut is fast, but throws away the shape of your uncertainty entirely — sometimes the safest action depends on the SPREAD of possibilities, not just the single best guess.
26SP-CS650-154HY · Artificial Intelligence · Prof. Fomba Kassoh

26.6 UNCERTAIN MOVEMENTS
Online Replanning: Model Predictive Control
THE CORE IDEA
Instead of committing to one long deterministic plan, plan for only a SHORT horizon — then replan from scratch at every single time step, using whatever new information just arrived.
● Effectively becomes a policy: at every step, run a planner, take only its FIRST action, then throw the rest of the plan away and replan.
● If new information arrives, or you end up somewhere unexpected — that's fine. You're about to replan anyway.
● Closely related to real-time search and game-playing algorithms (recall iterative deepening / limited-horizon minimax from earlier chapters).
REAL-WORLD ANCHOR
This is exactly what Waymo and Tesla's Autopilot are doing dozens of times per second on the highway — a short-horizon plan, executed for one instant, then thrown away and recomputed from scratch.

26.6 UNCERTAIN MOVEMENTS
The Value of Information Strikes Back
● Planning with only 'what I currently know' is really solving a fresh MDP at every step — that's separating estimation from control.
● But the true structure is a POMDP: the robot could take actions specifically to reduce uncertainty about the future.
● A robot planning from its current belief, and never accounting for future information it might gain, will never take an action that looks suboptimal right now but pays off hugely in information later.
– Example: a navigation robot could detour to a landmark just to firm up its position estimate — even if that landmark is 'out of the way' according to its current, incomplete knowledge.

Step 1 — move toward the surface
Step 2 — slide into the hole
● A guarded movement = a motion command + a termination condition (a predicate on sensor values that says when to stop).
● Peg-in-hole example: moving straight down risks landing on either side of the hole, with no way to tell which — but a SEQUENCE of guarded moves can guarantee success.

26.7 RL IN ROBOTICS
Why RL in Robotics Is Uniquely Hard
Continuous spaces
● Handled via discretization, or (more commonly) function approximation — features or deep neural networks.
● Neural nets skip manual feature engineering, but need far more data to train.
Real time, real consequences
● Simulated Go/chess training runs millions of games in hours. The real world refuses to move faster than one second per second.
● Real actions can permanently break things — no “undo.”
● Central challenge: minimize sample complexity — the number of real-world interactions needed to learn the task.

26.7.1 EXPLOITING MODELS
Model-Based RL and Its Variants
● Model-based RL: alternate between fitting dynamics parameters and computing a better policy from the fitted model.
– Even an imperfect physics model helps — researchers learn an ERROR TERM on top of the equations to compensate for what physics got wrong.
– Or skip the equations entirely: fit locally linear models, each valid in one small region of state space — this has successfully taught robots to master complex dynamic tasks like juggling.
SAFE EXPLORATION
Reducing samples is important, but reducing samples in DANGEROUS states matters even more — we don't want a robot falling off cliffs or breaking mugs while it learns. An approximate model, WITH quantified uncertainty, can constrain which actions the robot is even allowed to try.

26.7.1 EXPLOITING MODELS
Sim-to-Real Transfer & Domain Randomization
THE SIM-TO-REAL PROBLEM
Train in a fast, cheap simulator; deploy in the slow, expensive, unforgiving real world. The gap between the two is an active area of research.
● Add noise to the simulated model during training — makes the resulting policy more robust to model imperfections.
● Domain randomization: train across many DIFFERENT simulated parameter settings (friction, damping, even lighting/visual textures) so the policy generalizes rather than overfitting to one exact simulator.
CASE STUDY
OpenAI's dexterous-hand project trained a policy across thousands of randomized simulations — varying friction, lighting, object size — then deployed the SAME policy directly on a physical robot hand with real cameras and range finders, and it worked.

● Reuse the EXACT same trick from prediction — but for a different purpose.
– Before: assume noisy-rational human actions to PREDICT what they'll do next.
– Now: assume noisy-rational human DEMONSTRATIONS to infer WHAT COST FUNCTION explains those actions — then adopt that cost function as the robot's own.
WORKED EXAMPLE (FIG. 26.29)
A person drives a demonstration vehicle staying carefully on a dirt road, avoiding the grassy shoulder. The robot infers a cost function that penalizes leaving the road — and when placed in a BRAND NEW scene it's never seen, it correctly plans a path that also stays on the road there, because it learned the underlying PREFERENCE, not just the one demonstrated path.

26.8.2 LEARNING WHAT HUMANS WANT
Imitation Learning: Skip the Cost Function Entirely
Behavioral cloning
● Treat demonstrations as labeled data: D = {(xi, ui)}, then run ordinary supervised learning to fit a policy π: x → u.
● Challenge: generalization. The robot has no CAUSAL understanding of why an action was labeled optimal — it might fail on states just slightly off the demonstrated path.
The ALVINN case study
● Even starting exactly on the demonstrated path, small policy errors nudge the car slightly off it.
● Off the path, π has even LESS training data to draw on — errors compound, and the car drifts further and further away.

26.8.2 LEARNING WHAT HUMANS WANT
DAGGER: Fixing the Compounding-Error Problem
GENERAL
Start from a human expert demonstration. Learn policy π1. Roll it out to generate new data D — including states OFF the original demonstrated path.
SUBSTITUTE
Ask the human expert what THEY would have done at every state visited during that rollout, and add those labels to D.
RESULT
Train π2 on ALL data gathered so far (original + new). Repeat: πn generates data, human labels it, πn+1 is trained on everything. The robot learns to correct its own mistakes as they happen.

26.9 ALTERNATIVE FRAMEWORKS
Deliberative vs. Reactive Robotics
Deliberative (everything so far)
● Define or learn a reward/cost function.
● Have the robot OPTIMIZE it, via planning or learning.
● Sections 26.3 through 26.8 — all deliberative.
Reactive
● Skip modeling the world and planning entirely.
● The robot is a REFLEX agent: directly map sensor readings to actions.
● Sometimes it's just easier to build a good direct policy than to build an accurate world model.

26.9.1 REACTIVE CONTROLLERS
Genghis the Hexapod: A Reflex Agent With State
S1

Why not just plan?
● 6 legs × 2 motors each = 12 DOF for the legs alone, plus 6 more for position/orientation = 18-dimensional C-space.
● Sensors are too weak for accurate terrain models anyway.
● Even fast computers hit the curse of dimensionality here.
THE RULE
Choose a gait (e.g., move 3 legs, then the other 3). If a leg's forward swing gets blocked: retract it, lift it a bit higher, and try again. No terrain model required at all.

26.9.2 SUBSUMPTION ARCHITECTURE
Composing Reflexes Bottom-Up
● Augmented Finite State Machines (AFSMs): finite-state machines with sensor-conditioned transitions, message-passing arcs, and internal timers (clocks).
● The subsumption architecture (Brooks, 1986) provides primitives for SYNCHRONIZING multiple AFSMs and combining conflicting outputs.
● Composition strategy: start with one AFSM per leg, add an AFSM to coordinate all six legs together, then layer higher-level behaviors like collision avoidance on top.
WHY THIS IS GENUINELY IMPRESSIVE
Producing Genghis's walking behavior with configuration-space path planning would demand an accurate 18-dimensional terrain model AND handle stochastic effects like sliding on a slope. Subsumption sidesteps all of it — bottom-up composition of simple reflexes instead.

26.9.2 SUBSUMPTION ARCHITECTURE
Where Subsumption Struggles
● Problem 1 — brittle to unreliable sensors: AFSMs are driven by raw sensor input; if sensor data needs nontrivial integration over time, subsumption tends to fail.
● Problem 2 — no notion of changing goals: a subsumption robot usually does exactly one task, with no natural way to reprogram it for a new goal.
● Problem 3 — combinatorial explosion of conditions: a real policy for even one 'simple' maneuver can require considering many interacting factors.
WORKED EXAMPLE — THE LANE CHANGE THAT WON'T STAY SIMPLE
Start: 'nudge into the target lane.' Then: not every driver yields, so add 'wait for a response, then proceed or retreat.' Then: the right nudge speed depends on the target lane's speed, whether there's a car ahead in it, and whether there's a car behind you in YOUR lane. The condition list keeps growing — this is exactly why real autonomous-driving stacks blend reactive AND deliberative components rather than choosing purely one or the other.

CHAPTER SUMMARY
The Big Through-Line
● A robot is a physically embodied agent — its actions change the real world, which is unforgiving and refuses to run faster than real time.
● The general robotics problem is stochastic (MDPs), partially observable (POMDPs), and multi-agent (game theory) — often all at once, in continuous, high-dimensional spaces.
● In an ideal world: raw sensor feeds in, motor torques out, solved end to end. In practice: we decouple perception, motion planning, and control — trading some performance for tractability.
● The whole field is moving toward RE-integrating those pieces as our tools mature.
